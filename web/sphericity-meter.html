01 · Student challenge

Build a rule a stranger can use

A museum wants a way to compare its specimens and choose a representative rock from each collection. It expects a specimen you haven’t seen yet. Give the museum a procedure another group can follow without asking you what you meant.

  1. Propose. Rank three specimens by eye. Write down what your rule should notice and whether it describes one rock or the spread across a collection. Don’t pick a formula yet.
  2. Measure and repeat. Use a ruler, calipers or flat blocks, and, when appropriate, a graduated vessel and scale. Mark specimen IDs, define the directions and units, then have another operator reposition the rock and repeat. If you use displacement, state what you did about open cavities and bubbles.
  3. Compare and revise. Challenge the rule with a ball, a cube, and a flattened pebble. If the rule ties two objects that should differ for your purpose, find out what it misses. If the difference is no bigger than repeat-reading disagreement, report it as unresolved.
  4. Hand it off. Exchange written rules without coaching. Have the receiving group apply yours to a new specimen. Keep the first readings, document what the two groups did differently, and revise the instruction that caused the disagreement.

Hand in: the purpose and units, the setup and steps, original and repeat readings, one counterexample, the revised rule, and the held-out check. Name something your method cannot distinguish.

02 · A comparison after the first trial

A cube can tie a ball. What did your rule measure?

Sphericity is about resemblance to a sphere. Smoothness is about texture. Roundness describes the smoothing of corners and edges. A polished pebble can be smooth and have rounded edges while still being visibly flattened.

Reveal three benchmark comparisons after proposing your rule

Illustrative ideal geometry, not measured rocks. All dimensions are full perpendicular spans in millimeters. “Two-sphere” compares the equal-volume sphere’s diameter with the smallest enclosing sphere’s diameter.

The span ratio tests proportions and misses corners. The enclosure score considers how much of a bounding sphere the object fills. A cube’s enclosing diameter is its space diagonal, not the face-to-face span. Both scores are operational definitions. Wadell’s surface-area definition asks a different question and needs surface-area evidence.

Published evidence · a real wear run, not an Atlas run

In Domokos and colleagues’ 2014 abrasion experiment, the recorded spans of the limestone specimen in sheet exp_4LA were the same at 0 and 150 drum rotations: 70.92 × 60.53 × 51.50 mm. The volume estimated from mass fell from 221.26 to 142.74 cm³. What change would your rule miss if you measured spans alone? Follow the paper and its supporting spreadsheet.

What survives: a fully written teacher guide, generated figures, blank forms, and mathematical benchmarks. The guide proposes a classroom pilot. It reports no classroom outcomes or Atlas specimen-run data.

03 · Teacher notes

Delay the formula; make the handoff the test

The guide is designed for grades 9–12 or introductory undergraduate STEM in three 50-minute lessons, with an optional streambed investigation. Use pages 3–5 for setup and the sequence, pages 6–8 for the debrief, and pages 9–10 for the student sheet and pilot criteria. Collect the first version before revealing standard measures. Assess repeatability and whether a new team can use the rule, not agreement with a preferred index.

Young’s activity draws on the Model-Eliciting Activity tradition of Richard Lesh and colleagues. Students construct a model for a client, assess it themselves, document it, and revise it for reuse. Lesh and colleagues did not author or endorse this activity.

04 · Carry the idea forward

Uniform compared with what?

A set of equally flat pebbles can be uniform without being spherical. Separate a specimen’s departure from a reference, variation across specimens, and measurement repeatability. Then ask what a summary loses: rearranging the same stones into patches preserves their mean and spread but changes the pattern.

SQM+ Hallway Observatory asks a parallel question. Two walks can have the same mean step interval and spread but a different order of long and short intervals. In both projects a qualitative impression becomes measurements another person can inspect and challenge. This is a transfer of a measurement idea, not a claim about gait or rock data.
