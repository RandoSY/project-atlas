CORE 10 · Software-Defined Laboratory

Can a cup of water tell you how long something took?

Catch water during an event and weigh it. If the stream is steady and you know how many grams arrive each second, the cup becomes an interval timer. Then give that timer a harder job. Time a ball rolling down an incline.

01 · Physical experiment

Build the clock before you time the ball

Start with a reservoir, a small outlet, a collection cup, a scale, and a stopwatch. You can run the first version by hand. The instrumented version uses an original M5Stack ATOM Matrix and an I²C weighing module, configured to sample twice per second.

  1. Calibrate

    Tare the empty cup. Collect water over known stopwatch intervals, starting at the same reservoir level. Plot mass versus time. If the plot is close to a straight line, its slope gives the flow in grams per second.

  2. Test it

    Use the fitted flow to predict the duration of a separate collection. Compare with a stopwatch. Then let the reservoir drain and see whether the estimate drifts as the water level falls.

  3. Transfer to the ramp

    Collect only while the ball is moving. Release from rest, keep the ramp angle fixed, and repeat at several distances. Compare a plot of distance versus inferred time squared with an independent timing check.

Worked example, not a recorded trial At a steady 2 g/s, collecting 6 g takes 3 seconds. If the flow changes appreciably, use a calibration curve over the same reservoir conditions instead of dividing by a single rate.

02 · Recovered dashboard

The graph tests the clock, too

In the Analysis tab, a pattern in the residuals—the difference between each reading and the fitted line—can reveal a changing flow even when the mass plot looks nearly straight. The v0.2 kit is a bench-test candidate. The archive contains the lesson, software, and a test plan, but no verified physical run was recovered.

03 · Browser lab

From a stream to calculus

In the recovered Changing World lab, compare Ideal constant flow with Torricelli variable flow. Watch what happens to the slope of the mass curve as the reservoir empties. Then switch to Galileo Ramp and follow a second kind of accumulation, from acceleration to velocity to distance.
