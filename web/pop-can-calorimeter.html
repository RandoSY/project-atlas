A tea light loses mass. Water in a pop can warms up. Neither observation alone tells the whole story. Put a scale and a thermometer on the same clock, and you can begin to account for where the energy went.

ONE CANDLE · TWO MEASUREMENTS

A scale on the input side. A thermometer on the output side.

What did the candle use?
Measure the decrease in candle mass. Use a separate energy-per-gram reference if you need a fuel-energy estimate.
What did the water gain?
Measure the mass of the water and how much warmer it got. The water is the part of the system we can account for most directly.
What did time reveal?
Watch how both quantities change. Two runs can end at the same temperature but deliver that heat at different rates.

EXPERIENCE BEFORE EQUATION

One apparatus, six levels of inquiry

  1. Observe the change

    Before calculating anything, predict which quantity goes up and which goes down. Is temperature the same thing as energy? What if the amount of water doubles?

  2. Make two measurements you trust

    Check the scale with a known mass and allow it to settle. Check the probe against a reference and keep it clear of the can wall. Begin with water mass, start and finish temperatures, candle mass before and after, and elapsed time. A scale change smaller than its repeatability is not evidence of a reliable burn rate.

  3. Wire up the same experiment

    Use an eScale or HX711 load-cell channel for candle mass and a logging probe such as a DS18B20 for water temperature. Put both on a common time base, mark the heating interval, and record the signals with units. An independent stand keeps the can off the candle scale. Check a no-flame baseline and protect the scale and wiring from heat. The browser displays the readings. The instruments make them.

  4. Count joules and watts

    With water mass in grams, calculate Qwater ≈ mwater × 4.184 × ΔT in joules. Divide by seconds for the average net heating rate of the water, or by grams of candle mass lost for water heat captured per gram. These are three different quantities.

  5. Let the curves earn the calculus

    The slope of mass versus time gives a burn rate when its sign is reversed. The slope of water temperature, multiplied by mc, gives the rate of energy accumulation in the water. The area under that rate curve gives the net energy gained by the water. Estimate slope over a suitable window because sample-to-sample differences can amplify noise. Integrating a rate derived from the same temperature trace is a consistency check, not a second independent measurement.

  6. Account for what went elsewhere

    Some energy heats the can and stand, warms moving air, radiates outward, or leaves with evaporation. If you have a defensible value for the wax’s heat of combustion, estimate fuel energy from mass lost × that value. The ratio Qwater/Qfuel is the measured setup’s estimated capture fraction, not a measurement of the wax’s intrinsic energy content.

MEASURED ENDPOINTS · CANDLE-ONLY RUN

What the scale actually saw

The archived UMA KitchenLab Gravimetric Combustion Learning Guide (v5) records 0.695 g of mass loss over 12½ minutes in a candle-only run. That gives an average burn rate of about 0.0556 g/min. Using the guide’s classroom reference of 42 kJ per gram of paraffin gives an estimated fuel power of about 39 W. The scale measured mass, not watts. And those 39 W are not the heat captured by the water.

MODEL-ELICITING CHALLENGE

Which arrangement does more with a gram of fuel?

A new lab group wants a reliable way to compare two teacher-approved arrangements. Give them a protocol, not just an answer. Have teams change one variable, keep water mass and timing comparable, repeat runs, and report water heat per gram and their measurement uncertainty. Can a second team use the rule and reach the same conclusion? If two results are too close to distinguish with the instruments, say so.
