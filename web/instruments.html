How a board becomes an instrument

  1. SenseProbe, bridge, or sensor
  2. AcquireADC, clock, or digital bus
  3. CalibrateOn-board values and status
  4. SendUSB, BLE, or Wi-Fi
  5. UseRead, control, plot, export
  1. Get one trustworthy reading over USB first. Flash a Pico W build of MicroPython for simple slow sensors, or use Pico SDK C for the PIO counter. For ESP32, start with the exact board selected in Arduino/PlatformIO or ESP-IDF. Print the raw reading, calibrated value, units, and sensor status before writing the dashboard.
  2. Keep acquisition local. Time pulses and conversions on the board, not by browser arrival time. Start with one to five reports per second for these displays. Include a sequence number and device timestamp. Send an explicit no-signal or sensor-error state and mark old readings stale after disconnection.
  3. Pick the wireless path. For one nearby console, advertise a BLE UART service such as Nordic UART Service (NUS). The browser subscribes to telemetry notifications and writes permitted commands such as TARE; frame records by newline and reassemble BLE chunks before parsing. Use desktop Chrome or Edge for these Web Bluetooth examples. For Firefox or several viewers, have the instrument join the lab Wi-Fi as a station (STA), display its IP, and serve a read-only JSON endpoint and front panel on the same local origin. Start with polling; add SSE or WebSocket only if the experiment needs it.
  4. Measure, then prove the path. Check the instrument against something known, then compare USB and wireless results from the same sample. Verify updates after disconnect and reconnect. Keep the board and its control endpoints on the lab LAN. The public Atlas gallery is for viewing, not for reaching private devices.

01 / ELECTRICAL POTENTIAL

DC voltmeter (DVM)

Start with a known low-voltage source, then measure a battery while it is unloaded and under a small load. The historical Virtual DMM has more modes. This build is deliberately just a DC voltmeter.

@@dvm-controls@@ @@dvm-circuit@@ @@dvm-calibration@@
  1. Wire it. For a known 0–5 V DC bench source, use a 10 kΩ resistor from source positive to the ADC node and another 10 kΩ from that node to ground. Join source negative to board ground. The divider gives a nominal 0–2.5 V at the ADC. Check that this fits the board’s actual ADC range and ESP32 attenuation setting. The divider is not input protection. No mains, negative voltages, or unknown sources.
  2. Calibrate it. Record a shorted input and a known voltage measured with a reference meter, then fit gain and offset and check at a third point. On ESP32-S3 use ADC1 and the vendor’s ADC calibration support. Average slow readings and retain the raw ADC value for diagnostics.
  3. Show it. Transmit voltage in V. Hold freezes the display, not the hardware. Flag out-of-range readings instead of showing a plausible but wrong number.

02 / TIME AND COUNTING

Frequency meter

Start with a known 1 kHz, 3.3 V logic square wave. Watch how the reading changes as you change the gate time. A captured clock edge is the evidence. Wi-Fi and BLE merely carry the result.

@@frequency-meter@@ @@frequency-settings@@ @@frequency-usb-bench@@
  1. Wire it. Join grounds and use a clean 3.3 V logic source. A Pico W loopback can use PWM output GP15 to counter input GP14 in the documented bench pattern. For an external source, confirm the levels first and condition them as needed.
  2. Count it. On RP2040, use a PIO state machine to count rising edges while a hardware-derived gate runs. On ESP32-S3, use PCNT with a suitable glitch filter and handle counter limits. Do not assume PCNT is present on every ESP32 variant. Compute Hz = edges / gate_seconds. A 1 s gate gives 1 Hz count increments, not guaranteed 1 Hz accuracy. For slow signals, time successive edges and compute the reciprocal period instead.
  3. Prove it. A loopback checks the path, not absolute timebase accuracy when both sides share a clock. Check an independent known source too. Publish Hz, gate duration, and count, and report no signal after a defined timeout.

03 / FORCE INTO MASS

Electronic scale (eScale)

Put an empty cup on the platform, tare it, and add a known mass. Then use the same instrument to follow added water or evaporation over time.

@@scale-controls@@ @@scale-demo@@ @@scale-diagnostics@@
  1. Wire it. Mount a strain-gauge load cell according to its drawing. Connect its excitation and signal pairs to the HX711 breakout using the load-cell pinout, not guessed wire colors. Wire DOUT and SCK plus power and ground. Confirm 3.3 V logic compatibility. Some breakouts expose separate analog and digital supply pins.
  2. Calibrate it. Tare the empty platform or cup. Record a known mass after the readings settle, compute counts per gram from the difference, then verify with a different mass and check repeatability after unloading. Watch for drift and uneven mounting.
  3. Show it. Send grams, a stable/moving flag, and optionally raw counts. A tare command changes the zero, not the calibration slope. Keep demo and live visibly separate.

04 / DISTANCE WITHOUT A TAPE

Time-of-flight ruler

Point a VL53L1X at a matte card on a measured baseline. Move the card in steps and plot indicated distance minus the reference. Now try a dark or tilted target and compare. That's when a distance sensor becomes an exercise in measurement.

@@tof-pendulum@@
  1. Wire it. Choose a VL53L1X breakout rated for the board’s power and logic. Connect SDA, SCL, and common ground, with pull-ups to the correct logic supply. Use the board’s recommended power pin. The usual seven-bit I²C address is 0x29. The datasheet’s 0x52 notation is the shifted write address.
  2. Read it. Use the matching driver to start ranging and wait for data-ready. Record millimeters and the range status, and reject invalid returns before plotting. Keep a fixed reference plane and test several distances against a tape. Compare repeatability before and after changing target angle or lighting.
  3. Make it wireless. Replace the Nano/Web Serial transport shown above with Pico W or ESP32 acquisition and BLE or Wi-Fi telemetry. That is the proposed ruler path. The archived screenshot shows the real range-and-plot interface lineage, not a completed wireless ruler test.

05 / TEMPERATURE OVER TIME

Thermometer

Measure the cooling of a cup of warm water. A single temperature is a reading. Timestamps and a log turn it into a cooling curve you can compare across cups, lids, and starting conditions.

@@thermometer-lab12@@ @@thermometer-unoq@@
  1. Wire it. Use a powered, three-wire DS18B20. Connect VDD to 3.3 V, GND to ground, and DQ to the selected GPIO. Add a typical 4.7 kΩ pull-up from DQ to 3.3 V unless the breakout already has one. Use an immersion-rated probe if it goes into water, and keep the connector and board dry.
  2. Read it. Start conversion, wait for completion (up to 750 ms at 12-bit resolution), read and check the scratchpad CRC, and timestamp the result on the board. Don't transmit the boot-time default as a live reading. For a cooling curve, a reading every few seconds is usually plenty.
  3. Prove it. Compare in a stirred bath against a reference thermometer and capture ambient temperature separately. Log °C and device time over BLE or Wi-Fi. Keep missing samples visible, and hold probe depth constant so you know what changed between trials.
