Can a cheap range sensor map a corner if you give it enough good observations? A single echo tells you how far away something is. Add a bearing and a known starting position, and that echo becomes a point on a map. The trick is knowing which part of the map to trust.

01 · Try it

Can you tell a noisy wall from a crooked map?

  1. Load Kitchen. In Sensor Model, set Noise ± cm and Wall echo loss % to zero. In Fault Lab, click Reset faults. Make a prediction about where the dots should land.
  2. Run a Virtual 360° scan. On Arena, compare the scan points with the drawn walls and obstacles. On Data you can inspect the bearings and distances, or use Export CSV to save a copy.
  3. Go back to Arena, click Clear scan, set Heading offset ° to 15, and scan again without moving the robot. Do the dots get fuzzier, or does the pattern shift together? Can you explain why?
  4. For a second comparison, reset faults and restore some noise (try ±10 cm). Clear the scan between runs. Compare 1 sample per reading with 7, keeping the geometry and other settings fixed. Repeat both runs before drawing a conclusion.

What you’re looking at. The drawn room is the simulator’s known geometry. The plotted points are estimates generated from simulated readings. The exported scan CSV from this lab contains simulated data, not measurements made by a robot.

03 · Test the explanation

The map depends on where you think you’re pointing

Range
The physical design uses an ultrasonic echo, not a scanning laser. Range tells you how far away a reflecting surface appears to be, but not which surface made the echo.
Bearing and position
Use the measured range to place a dot along the reported bearing from a known position. If the heading is biased, even a perfectly repeatable range gets plotted in the wrong place.
Repeated readings
A median can reduce the effect of an odd reading. It cannot fix a misaligned heading, a wide acoustic beam, or a wrong starting position.
A second viewpoint
A corner hidden from one spot may be visible from another. Mark both starting positions in the same coordinate system before comparing scans. This is not simultaneous localization and mapping.

Randall Young’s patient approach makes the measurement process visible. Instead of asking only whether a map looks right, ask whether you know where each point came from and which observations another group could reproduce.

04 · Teach it or build it

Give another team enough evidence to place the corner

Student challenge. Pick two marked positions in a known layout. Agree on a reference direction. Choose a rule for taking and rejecting readings. Hand a second team your sketch, ranges, and bearings without showing them your finished map. Can they place the same corner? Which observation would settle a disagreement?

Bench extension · Proposed protocol. Start with the documented micro:bit v2, CuteBot, and directional ultrasonic ranger design. Keep the robot stationary at each marked pose while taking readings at known headings. Start with a flat target and compare with a tape measure. Record single and repeated ranges, heading, and any missing echo. Move to the second pose only after documenting the first. Compare the two outlines and keep poor readings in the record rather than silently deleting them.

Teacher move. Give groups the same room geometry but different errors to diagnose. Ask them to name the evidence they’d need to distinguish a heading problem from a noisy sensor. This connects to Virtual Instruments, where calibration and context make a reading meaningful, and to The Singing Rule, where a simple object makes a model visibly testable.
