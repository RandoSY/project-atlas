CORE 10 · Kitchen Inquiry Lab

Can you tell how strong the tea is without tasting it?

Make a series of dilutions from one cup of tea. Shine the same light through each sample and compare what gets through. Then see whether your calibration can identify an unlabeled sample from that same cup.

01 · Physical experiment

Start with a blank. End with an unknown.

  1. Make standards

    Use one filtered, cooled stock tea and the same water for the blank and all dilutions. For a first pass, try 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% stock by volume. Keep the cuvette type, fill height, and orientation fixed.

  2. Measure light

    Take a dark reading with the LED off. With the LED on and settings unchanged, read the water blank and the samples. Check the blank again at the end to see whether the source or detector drifted.

  3. Predict an unknown

    Plot the log-transformed reading against the known stock fractions. Fit only the range that behaves approximately linearly, then estimate an unlabeled dilution inside that range. Check the prediction against how the sample was actually made.

Two ways to build it. The original low-cost path used a lamp or LED, a light-dependent resistor, and a digital voltmeter. That is a good comparison instrument, but divider voltage is not directly proportional to light. The documented upgrade pairs one LED color at a time and 10 mm cuvettes with a VEML7700 detector, fixed gain and integration time, and serial logging.

02 · Experience before equation

The detector sees light. The calibration estimates strength.

T = (sample − dark) / (blank − dark)A = −log10(T)

With fixed path length and wavelength, ideal Beer–Lambert absorbance rises in proportion to concentration. The transmitted light does not. For example, 50% transmission gives A ≈ 0.301, while 25% gives A ≈ 0.602. Each additional 0.301 of A halves the transmitted light.

What can you claim? Tea is a mixture, and particles can scatter light. Treat A as an optical attenuation index, or use your standards to report an equivalent fraction of this particular stock tea. It does not tell you the caffeine concentration or how strong another tea will taste. IUPAC definition.
